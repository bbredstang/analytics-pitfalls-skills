# The Unicorn Delusion

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/31

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/31

## The problem

Believing you need rare "unicorn" team members who are experts at everything—business + technology + communication + design—and never investing in building people up. Leaders look at those Venn diagrams and say "that's just unrealistic!" then never develop their team. You stay stuck hunting for mythical creatures that don't exist, posting job descriptions nobody can fill, complaining you "can't find good people." Meanwhile, you're ignoring the goldmine sitting right in front of you: people who could become unicorns with six months of coaching.

## The principle

BUILD UNICORNS, DON'T HUNT THEM: Unicorns aren't born. They're built. In six months you can turn a business analyst into someone who builds solutions, or a technical developer into someone who can do requirements.

Two proven paths:

PATH 1 - BA → Builder: Business analysts already understand the business. Give them modern tools (Tableau, PowerBI) and minor coaching. They have less muscle memory to overcome and will run laps around decade-long veterans stuck in old ways.

PATH 2 - Developer → Business Partner: Get technical folks using design-first approaches and mockups for requirements. When they start doing templated designs early, magic happens. They build REALLY fast because nothing gets lost in translation.

The compound effect: What if 20% of your team becomes unicorns? Or 50%? Even getting ONE person is worth it. Identify willing learners, provide modern tools, coach don't gatekeep, and celebrate their "personal average" improving month over month. 

Stop posting unicorn job descriptions. Start building unicorns from the people you already have. It's faster, cheaper, and actually works.

## The steps

1. Identify 3 Willing Learners: Who on your team wants to grow? BAs who want to build? Developers who want to understand business? Start there.
2. Give Them Modern Tools + Coaching: Tableau for BAs. Design-first frameworks for developers. Weekly 30-min coaching sessions. Remove barriers. Celebrate small wins.
3. Track Their Personal Average: Measure their output monthly. Are they improving? Shipping more? Building confidence? Make progress visible. Double down on what works.

## In practice

Matt joined the team as a business analyst. Background: degree in Anthropology, previous job translating technical manuals. Zero coding experience. Zero BI tool experience. The team needed builders, not translators.

The manager made a bet: "What if we give Matt Tableau and see what happens?"

Four months later, Matt was building production dashboards that rivaled the work of 10-year veterans. How? He wasn't held back by "this is how we've always done it" muscle memory. He learned the modern way from day one. No bad habits to unlearn.

Meanwhile, Sarah—a senior developer who'd been coding for 15 years—started using mockups for requirements gathering. She'd sketch wireframes before writing code. Projects that used to take six months started finishing in six weeks because nothing got lost in translation. She WAS the requirements gatherer AND the builder.

Two unicorns. Both built, not hired. Both transformed the team.

The manager stopped posting "must have 10 years experience in everything" job descriptions. Started hiring willing learners and building them up. Team velocity tripled in a year.
