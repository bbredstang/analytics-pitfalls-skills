# We Forgot People Use This Stuff (The Tech Was Perfect, Adoption Was Zero)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/14

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
2. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
3. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
4. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
5. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
6. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/14

## The problem

You build a brilliant analytics platform. The data model is perfect. The queries are blazing fast. The UI is... well, it works if you know SQL and understand star schemas. You launch it. Adoption: 8%. Why? Because you forgot that humans have to use it. Analysts are confused. Business users are intimidated. Nobody got trained. Nobody asked for feedback during development. You optimized for technical excellence and forgot about the people. It's like designing a car with a perfect engine but no steering wheel—technically impressive, functionally useless. Technology is only valuable when people actually use it.

## The principle

Design for humans, not for perfection. Talk to users early and often. Build for their skill level, not yours. Provide training, documentation, and support. Celebrate adoption, not technical complexity. Think manufacturing: a perfect machine that operators can't run is worthless. The magic happens when people come together around tools they understand and love. When adoption is high because the solution fits their workflow, not because you forced it. Less "look how smart we are," more "look how easy this is to use." That's what drives impact.

## The steps

1. Shadow Users for a Day: Watch real people use your tools. Don't explain anything. Just observe. You'll see where they struggle, where they give up, what confuses them.
2. Build Training INTO the Product: Don't make training a separate thing. Build tooltips, onboarding flows, example use cases right into the UI. Make it self-explanatory.
3. Track Adoption AND Satisfaction: Measure both usage (are they using it?) and satisfaction (do they like it?). Send quick surveys: "How easy was this? 1-5." Iterate based on feedback.

## In practice

A healthcare system built a patient analytics platform with every metric imaginable. Data engineers loved it. Doctors hated it—too complex, too many clicks, required training they didn't have time for. Adoption: 11%. Platform gathered dust. New CTO brought in a UX designer (gasp!). They shadowed doctors for a week. Learned what they actually needed: 3 key metrics, one-click access, mobile-friendly. Rebuilt the interface—same data, radically simpler UI. Launched with training sessions and on-call support. Adoption jumped to 68% in 8 weeks. Doctors started using data to adjust treatment plans. Patient outcomes improved. Cost per patient dropped 9%. The tech didn't change—the human experience did. That's where the magic is.
