# Licenses and Lip Service (When Everyone Logs In and Nobody Changes)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Second Factory*, Brian Cariveau's next novel (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/second-factory).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/67

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
2. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
3. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
4. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
5. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/67

## What it sounds like at work

"Everyone’s got a license, and almost everyone finished the training."

## The problem

You bought AI licenses for the whole company and built a course people can take at their own pace. Three weeks later the dashboard is a thing of beauty: 94% logged in, 88% finished the training. The CEO forwards it with three exclamation points.

Then you ask people one question in the lunch line. What did you use it for this week? An email to a vendor. A summary of a long thread. A chicken recipe. "I haven't really had time yet. I did the training, though."

Logins and completions are true numbers. They just don't tell you whether anyone works differently. Somebody played the whole course at double speed in another tab and picked the longest answer on every quiz. Indifference looks exactly like compliance, and it never shows up in the logs.

## The principle

Usage is the receipt. Logins, licenses and course completions are promises. Daily use on real work is proof.

Rolling out AI looks like a technical problem: buy the tools, run the course, watch the numbers climb. Ronald Heifetz would call it an adaptive challenge. People have to change their habits, and nobody can install a habit for them.

So measure the receipt gap: the share of people with access, minus the share using it every working day. Then close it the slow way. Sit beside people while they work on a real problem of their own. Get someone people watch to stand up in the front row. And never put the login number on a board slide without the daily-use number next to it.

## The steps

1. **Take the receipt gap this week.** Access minus daily use. An active day is one real interaction, not a login. A daily user is active every working day. Put both numbers side by side wherever the login number shows up today.
2. **Ask the lunch-line question.** Ask twenty people at random: what did you use it for this week? Count how many name a real piece of their own work. That answer shows you the indifference the logs miss.
3. **Trade a module for a sit-down.** Run a three-hour Bring Your Own Problem session. Everyone brings one real problem from their job and works on it in the room, with helpers who never grab the keyboard. Follow up with every person within 48 hours.
4. **Put someone visible in the front row.** Don't fill the first room with enthusiasts. Invite a respected skeptic, someone people watch. When they stand up, the room follows.

## In practice

At ClassicMotors, a 900-person classic car marketplace, the CEO stood up at the all-hands and announced the company would be AI native. Licenses for everyone on Monday. A six-module course. A hundred percent completion in thirty days. Behind him, a dashboard with two numbers: licenses activated, training completed.

Three weeks in, the numbers were 94% and 88%. He was putting them on the first slide for the board.

Nora Berg, the senior manager he'd put in charge, asked the data team for one more number. Not logins. How many people used the tools every working day? Eleven percent. And 34 of those had simply scheduled an inbox summary for six every morning, which most of them never opened.

Module four of the course was called Building Agentic Workflows with Skills, Tools and Retrieval-Augmented Generation. A campaign manager finished all six modules in one afternoon and told her he still didn't know what he'd use it for.

The numbers on the CEO's slide were true. They just didn't tell him anything. Nora told him: "I watched you fall in love with a demo once. I'm not going to let you fall in love with a dashboard."
