# Dead Weight Climbers

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/33

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
2. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
3. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
4. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
5. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
6. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/33

## The problem

Trying to drive transformation when your team doesn't actually want to make the climb together. The mountaintop is clear. Everyone can see the peak. But transformation requires SIGNIFICANT effort—like real mountain climbing with planning, coordination, and teamwork. You look around and ask "do you all want to do this together?" The answer you get? Silence. Fake smiles. Or worse, "I guess so." Every person who doesn't want to climb becomes dead weight, holding everyone back. You'll stall halfway up the mountain wondering why you feel stuck in the mud.

## The principle

PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY + GENUINE EXCITEMENT = MOMENTUM: Transformation requires four things, not one or two, but all four: (1) A clear mountaintop everyone can see (2) A documented plan that gives psychological safety about what's coming (3) Genuine excitement—not forced enthusiasm (4) Shared commitment to climb together. Without all four, you're doomed from the start.

Get explicit commitment early: Don't assume people are on board. Ask "are you in?" and actually LISTEN to the answer. If they're not excited, figure out WHY before starting. Maybe they need more context. Maybe they need a different role. Maybe they need to leave the team. That's okay.

Create the plan on paper: Detailed enough to give psychological safety about the journey. People won't climb if they don't know what's ahead.

Address dead weight directly: Have honest conversations early. Starting smaller with a fully committed team of 8 beats starting big with a half-hearted team of 20. Momentum beats headcount when everyone wants to climb.

## The steps

1. Get Explicit Commitment: 1-on-1s with everyone. Ask: "Are you in? Do you want to do this?" Listen to the answer. No fake enthusiasm. Real commitment or real exit.
2. Document the Plan: Write the transformation plan on paper. Detailed enough for psychological safety. Milestones, timelines, roles. Make the path visible.
3. Create Early Wins: First quarter should prove "this works." Ship something valuable fast. Build momentum. Prove to skeptics that the climb is worth it.

## In practice

A data platform migration had everything: executive support, a two-year roadmap, budget approved, kickoff celebrations. Six months in? Way behind schedule. Way over budget. Team morale in the toilet.

The director couldn't figure it out. "We have everything we need! Why aren't we making progress?"

Then someone did exit interviews with three people who quit. The truth came out: 40% of the team never bought in. Some were vocally resistant in meetings. Most were quietly resistant—nodding in meetings, doing nothing after.

The team had dead weight climbers. They were carrying people who didn't want to make the climb.

New leader came in. Had brutally honest one-on-ones with everyone. "Are you in? Do you actually want to do this?" Some people admitted: "No, I don't believe in this direction." Okay. They left or moved to other teams. Some recommitted: "I was unclear on the plan. Now I get it. I'm in." Great.

The team went from 20 half-committed people to 12 fully bought-in climbers. They finished the remaining 18 months of work in 14 months. Better quality. Zero drama.

Momentum beats headcount. Always. When everyone wants to climb, you move fast.
