# The Porsche at the Front (When the Digital Side Outruns the Factory)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Second Factory*, Brian Cariveau's next novel (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/second-factory).

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
2. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
3. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
4. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
5. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/81

## The problem

AI has made everything digital dramatically faster. Design, catalog, storefront and marketing are each having their best month ever. And on the other side of all of it, at the factory, the warehouse, the clinic or the call center, the orders pile up.

Every morning the physical side gets another email saying something else is urgent. So it pulls a half-finished job, sets up another, and the half-made work sits on a cart. Rushing makes quality worse. Customers start asking where their order is. Every team is having its best month, and the business is having its worst.

The digital side can now move at extraordinary speed. Somewhere in almost every business there's a physical world that can't. When the fast side outruns it, all it makes is a pile-up.

## The principle

Put the slowest car at the front. The whole system goes as fast as its slowest step, so let that step set the pace for everything.

Eliyahu Goldratt's The Goal has a scout troop on a hike and a boy named Herbie who walks slower than everyone else. The troop can only reach camp as fast as Herbie walks, so you put Herbie at the front. Fast stations ahead of the constraint add gaps and piles, and no speed at all.

So find out how fast the physical side really goes, and write it down where your agents can read it. Don't sell faster than you can make it right. Stop jerking the constraint around with daily emergencies. Then spend your effort making the slow step faster, like checking the design before you cut the metal. It will feel wrong to ask Marketing to sell less this month. It's right.

## The steps

1. **Find your Bel Air.** Where does the digital side hand off to something physical: a factory, a warehouse, a truck, a clinic, a call center? How much can it really do in a week?
2. **Write capacity down where agents can read it.** How many units can be made, to standard, in a given week. Every agent and campaign that makes a promise reads from it.
3. **Sell only as fast as you can deliver.** Honest dates, and a waiting list for the rest.
4. **Lock the plan.** Plan the physical side two weeks at a time, and leave it alone. No daily emergencies.
5. **Catch problems before they're made.** Check the design before you cut the metal, instead of finding out on Friday what went wrong on Monday.

## In practice

Three weeks after a runaway weekend, everything digital at ClassicMotors.parts was working beautifully. Scanning was producing forty new digital models a week, up from twelve. The storefront was live. Marketing had a spring campaign ready for thirty new parts. At the factory in Stockton: 11,940 orders waiting, 612 parts made last week, 449 passed inspection, and 1,104 emails asking where a part was.

Walt, who owned the factory, explained it from his catwalk. "Every morning I get an email saying something else is urgent. So I pull the Bronco job half finished and set up the Chevelle job, and the half-finished Bronco hinges sit on a cart for a week. And when we rush, quality goes to hell."

That Saturday, on the company's spring car rally, the fast cars at the front pulled away on the mountain curves. Halfway back, a 1958 Bel Air crawled up every hill with its temperature gauge creeping toward red. By the top, the convoy was in four pieces spread over six miles. The rally marshal walked up to the Bel Air's owner. "Phyllis, honey. You're going to lead us down the other side."

"Me? I'm the slowest one."

"That's exactly why."
