# Ice Cream Causes Drowning (When Numbers Lie)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
2. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
3. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
4. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
5. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/4

## The problem

VP of Sales storms into your office: "I ran the numbers—our email campaigns drive revenue! Every time we send emails, sales go up. Send more emails!" Sounds great, right? Except you dig deeper and find out sales go up on Mondays. You send emails on Mondays. Sales would've gone up anyway. The emails did nothing. But leadership is already budgeting for a bigger email team. It's the classic trap: ice cream sales correlate with drowning deaths. Both happen in summer. One doesn't cause the other. But if you don't understand causation, you might ban ice cream to save lives. Misunderstanding correlation leads to terrible decisions, wasted budget, and looking like an idiot when it doesn't work.

## The principle

Always ask: "What else could explain this relationship?" Correlation is a clue, not proof. To establish causation, you need controlled experiments—A/B tests, holdout groups, something that isolates the variable. Think manufacturing: if quality improves after you change suppliers, is it the new supplier? Or is it because you also trained staff that week? You need to isolate variables to know what actually works. The magic happens when you stop jumping to conclusions and start designing experiments. Less guessing, more testing. That's how you turn data into decisions you can trust.

## The steps

1. Ask "What Else?" Before You Act: Anytime you see correlation, write down 3 other possible explanations. Force yourself to consider alternatives before jumping to conclusions.
2. Run A/B Tests on Big Bets: Before you invest in something based on correlation, test it. Holdout groups, controlled experiments, anything that isolates the variable.
3. Build a "Causation Checklist": Create a simple checklist your team uses before acting on data: Did we isolate the variable? Did we test it? Do we have a plausible mechanism? No shortcuts.

## In practice

A retailer noticed ice cream sales correlated perfectly with drowning deaths in their beach town markets. Data team built a model: "Reduce ice cream promotions to reduce drowning risk." Marketing almost cut ice cream displays. Then someone asked: "What else happens when ice cream sales spike?" Summer. Hot weather. More people at the beach. They ran an A/B test: some stores cut ice cream displays, others didn't. Drowning rates? Identical. Ice cream sales in test stores? Down 30%. Revenue hit. Turns out the real cause was beach traffic, not ice cream. They reversed course, ran beach safety campaigns instead, and kept selling ice cream. Revenue recovered. Lives saved. That's the difference between correlation and causation.
