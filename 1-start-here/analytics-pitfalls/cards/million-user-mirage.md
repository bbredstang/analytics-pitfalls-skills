# The Million User Mirage (Celebrating the Wrong Wins)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/2

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/2

## The problem

Board meeting. "We hit 1 million users!" Champagne pops. Everyone celebrates. Three months later you're laying off half the team because revenue is in the toilet. Turns out 97% of those "users" signed up, looked around for 30 seconds, and never came back. You were measuring signups when you should've been measuring engagement. Revenue. Retention. The stuff that actually pays the bills. Or your data team celebrates "We built 47 dashboards this year!" Leadership claps. Until someone asks: "How many have 5+ weekly active users?" Silence. Turns out 28 of those 47 dashboards have 2 or fewer users. Some have zero. It's like a manufacturing plant celebrating "we made 1 million widgets!" while ignoring that 970,000 are sitting unsold in a warehouse. Vanity metrics feel good but they're a trap—they let you lie to yourself about success while the business bleeds out.

## The principle

Track what matters, not what looks good on a slide. Ask: "If this metric goes up, does revenue go up? Do customers get happier? Does the business actually win?" If the answer is no, it's a vanity metric—kill it. Focus on the manufacturing mindset: what's the yield? What percentage of raw materials (users, leads, dashboards) become finished products (paying customers, repeat buyers, dashboards with 5+ weekly users)? Measure the conversion at every step of your production line. Better yet: pick HALO METRICS that force fundamentals. Instead of tracking 20 things, track 3 things that FORCE you to get the other 17 right. You can't game a halo metric—it only improves when fundamentals improve. Examples: "85% of solutions used weekly" forces you to build things people want. "Average time idea→production: 3 weeks" forces you to eliminate waste. The magic happens when your whole team obsesses over the metrics that connect directly to business outcomes. Less is more—pick 3-5 metrics that matter and make them the center of every conversation.

## The steps

1. **Map Your Conversion Funnel**

Draw it out - from signup to paying customer to retained customer (or from dashboard built to dashboard with 5+ weekly users).

Calculate conversion % at each step. Identify the biggest drop-off.

Ask: "What percentage of raw materials become finished products?" If <10%, you have a vanity metrics problem.
2. **Pick 3-5 Halo Metrics That Matter**

For each potential metric, ask: "If this goes up, does revenue go up? Do customers get happier? Can I game this by cutting corners?"

If you can game it or it doesn't drive business outcomes, it's vanity. Kill it.

Best metrics:
- Force fundamentals (can't improve without fixing root issues)
- Measure outcomes, not activity
- Are un-gameable

Examples:
- "85% of solutions used weekly" (forces quality)
- "Average idea→production: 3 weeks" (forces efficiency)
- "Net revenue retention >110%" (forces customer success)
3. **Weekly Metric Review Ritual**

Every Monday, review your 3-5 halo metrics as a team.

Ask:
- "What changed?"
- "Why?"
- "What are we doing about it?"

Make the uncomfortable visible. Best metrics reveal problems, not hide them.

If a metric consistently shows green with no effort, it's probably vanity. Replace it with something harder that forces improvement.

## In practice

A SaaS startup celebrated hitting 500K signups. Investors loved it. Then burn rate caught up—they had 3 months of runway left. New CEO asked one question: "How many of those 500K are paying us?" Answer: 4,200. That's a 0.8% conversion rate. They pivoted hard. Stopped measuring signups. Started measuring: Free trial → paid conversion, Monthly active users, Customer LTV, Churn rate, Net revenue retention. Turned out their onboarding sucked—87% of free trials never completed setup. They fixed onboarding, conversion jumped to 12%. Revenue tripled in 6 months with the same traffic. The vanity metric almost killed them. The real metrics saved them.

Meanwhile, a data team loved their metrics: "We built 47 dashboards this year!" Team got bonuses. New VP asked: "How many of those 47 have 5+ weekly active users?" They ran the numbers. Brutal: 19 dashboards had 5+ users, 28 had 2 or fewer. VP changed the metric: "Dashboards with 5+ weekly active users for 3+ consecutive months." First month: 40% pass rate. Team panicked. "This is too hard!" VP: "Good. It should be hard. It forces us to build things people actually want." Team shifted focus: spent time understanding user needs, simpler designs, better data quality, actual user testing. Month 6: 82% pass rate. Bonus: they built 60% fewer dashboards but delivered 3x the business value. Quality over quantity. Usage over activity. The halo metric forced everything else to improve.
