# The Corvette Meeting

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/41

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/41

## The problem

Not doing the math on people's time. At a small consulting shop, every hour-long meeting with 4 people was "$145/hr × 4 = $580. I hope it was worth it." That comment stuck with you. But in big corporate at large enterprises? Weekly calls with 30, 40+ people achieving nothing. The only thing playing in your head: "With the cost of people on this call, we could buy a nice used Corvette. Except none of us get to drive it. We just burned it in a conference room." Same thing happens when teams spend six months evaluating something without actually testing a real market.

## The principle

DO THE MATH, THEN ACT RUTHLESSLY: Every person-hour costs real money. At enterprise scale with loaded costs (salary + benefits + overhead), you're looking at $100-300/hour per person. A 1-hour meeting with 40 people? That's $4,000-$12,000. A weekly meeting? $200K-600K annually. For what outcome?

Make the invisible visible: Calculate the actual cost. A 30-person weekly status meeting = 1,560 person-hours/year = $150K-450K. Could you hire 2-3 people to solve the problem instead? Could you buy the tool that eliminates the need?

Small company discipline at big company scale: Ask "Is this worth a Corvette?" before scheduling. Require clear outcomes for any meeting over 5 people. Default to async updates instead of synchronous meetings. If you DO meet, ruthless facilitation—agenda required, no laptops, decisions made.

Same principle for analysis paralysis: Evaluating something for 6 months with 10 people = $200K+ spent without testing a real market. A 3-week pilot with 2 people costs $15K and gives you actual data.

Do the math. Move faster.

## The steps

1. Do the Math This Week: Calculate cost of your 3 biggest recurring meetings. People × Hours × $150/hr. If any costs >$5K/month, justify it or kill it.
2. Implement the 5-Person Rule: Any meeting with 5+ people requires: (a) Written agenda sent 24hrs ahead, (b) A decision to be made, (c) Designated facilitator. No exceptions.
3. Default to Async: Make "could this be an email?" your first question. Track what % of meetings get converted to async. Target 60%+. Reserve sync meetings for decisions.

## In practice

At a major bank, the leader sat in weekly "alignment" calls. Forty-plus people from data teams across the company. No decisions made. Just updates everyone could have read in an email.

He did the math: 52 weeks × 40 people × 1 hour × $150/hr loaded cost = $312,000 per year. Enough to hire two senior analysts who could actually solve problems.

At a Fortune 5 healthcare company, a vendor evaluation dragged on for six months. Fifteen people in weekly meetings debating features they could have tested in a pilot. Cost: roughly $300,000 in time. A four-week paid pilot with the top two vendors would have cost $40,000 and provided real usage data from actual users.

The math was never done. The Corvette was burned.

Contrast: at the small consulting shop, every meeting started with "Is this worth what we're billing?" Most meetings became 15-minute standups. Some got canceled entirely because nobody could justify the cost.

The CEO's comment stuck: "$145/hr times four people. I hope it was worth it."

In big corporate, nobody asks. They should.
