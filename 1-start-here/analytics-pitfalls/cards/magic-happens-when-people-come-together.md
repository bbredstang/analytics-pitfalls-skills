# Magic Happens When People Come Together (The Anti-Silo Manifesto)

A principle card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/24

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work could use this principle.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/24

## The problem

You know what doesn't create magic? Smart people working in isolation. Doesn't matter how brilliant your data scientists are if they never talk to the analysts. Doesn't matter how good your engineers are if they don't understand what business users actually need. Silos kill innovation. They create redundant work—three teams building the same thing without knowing it. They create friction—endless handoffs and "that's not my job" moments. They create misalignment—everyone optimizing their piece without seeing the whole. It's like a manufacturing plant where each department optimizes locally and the overall line grinds to a halt. The magic is in the connections, not the components.

## The principle

Build a culture where cross-functional collaboration is the default, not the exception. Create regular rituals where different teams sync—data engineers with analysts, analysts with business users, everyone with leadership. Break down walls physically (co-locate teams) and virtually (shared Slack channels, joint OKRs). Celebrate wins that required collaboration—shine a light on teams working together. The magic happens when people help one another, enrich each other's work, and build something bigger than any individual could alone. That's when work becomes fun and impact becomes massive. Less "my team vs your team," more "one mission, many skills."

## The steps

1. Create Weekly Cross-Team Sync: Data engineers + analysts + business users. 30 minutes. Share what you're working on. Ask for help. Build relationships.
2. Co-Locate Teams Physically or Virtually: Shared Slack channels, joint OKRs, same office space if possible. Break down walls. Make collaboration the default.
3. Celebrate Collaborative Wins: When teams work together and ship something great, shine a light on it. Make heroes of collaborators, not lone wolves.

## In practice

A CPG company had data science building models, analytics building dashboards, and IT managing infrastructure—zero collaboration. Models sat unused because analysts didn't know they existed. Dashboards were slow because IT didn't optimize infrastructure. Redundant work everywhere. New CDO instituted "Integration Fridays"—every team sends someone to a 2-hour cross-functional working session. Data scientists learned what analysts needed. Analysts learned what IT could do. IT learned what would move the business. Six months later: data scientists embedded models directly into dashboards analysts already used (adoption 10x), IT pre-built optimized pipelines for common use cases (speed 5x faster), redundant projects dropped from 12 to 2. But here's the best part: teams started grabbing lunch together, friendships formed, people loved coming to work. That's the magic.
