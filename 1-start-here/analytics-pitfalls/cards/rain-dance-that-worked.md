# The Rain Dance That Worked (When You Credit the Wrong Thing)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/11

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/11

## What it sounds like at work

"Sales went up right after we launched it, so it worked."

## The problem

Sales team starts a new outreach campaign in January. Revenue spikes in February. VP of Sales declares victory: "Our campaign worked! Do more of it!" Except revenue spikes every February—it's tax refund season. The campaign did nothing. But the team doubles down, wastes budget, and wonders why results don't repeat. It's the rain dance fallacy: you do a rain dance, it rains, you assume the dance worked. Ignore that it was already cloudy. Humans are wired to find patterns and assign credit, even when it's coincidence. In business, this leads to investing in things that don't work and ignoring things that do. It's expensive and dumb.

## The principle

Before you credit success to your action, ask: "What would have happened anyway?" Use control groups, historical baselines, and holdout tests to separate your impact from external factors. Think manufacturing: if defect rates drop after you change suppliers, is it the supplier? Or is it the new QA process you also implemented? Isolate variables. The magic happens when you know what actually drives results so you can do more of it and less of everything else. Less guessing, more isolating. That's how you spend budget wisely.

## The steps

1. Check Historical Baselines: Before crediting your action, look at what happened in prior years. If sales spike in December, check last December. Separate your impact from seasonality.
2. Use Control Groups: For big initiatives, test with a control group. Half get the treatment, half don't. Measure the difference. That's your true incremental impact.
3. Build an "Impact Checklist": Before celebrating success, ask: What would have happened anyway? Did we isolate our impact? Do we have a control? No shortcuts on causality.

## In practice

A retail chain launched a loyalty program in November. December sales were up 30%. CMO presented to the board: "Our loyalty program is a massive success!" Allocated $5M to expand it nationally. But someone checked: December sales were up 30% last year too—holiday season. They ran a test: half the stores got the loyalty program, half didn't. Results? Stores with loyalty: +31%. Stores without: +29%. The program added 2%, not 30%. Holiday season did the heavy lifting. They scaled back the rollout, optimized the program, and actually measured incremental lift. Next iteration added 8%. That was worth scaling. Saved $3M by not over-investing in something that barely worked. All because they isolated causality instead of celebrating coincidence.
