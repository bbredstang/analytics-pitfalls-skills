# We're in the Behavior Change Business (Not the Data Business)

A principle card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/23

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work could use this principle.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/23

## The problem

Here's the truth nobody wants to hear: getting people to use data is no different than getting consumers to buy products. You're not selling widgets, you're selling behavior change. "Use this dashboard instead of that spreadsheet." "Trust this model instead of your gut." "Make decisions from data instead of opinions." That's behavior change. And behavior change is hard. It requires the same principles that get consumers to switch brands: make it easy, make it valuable, make it sticky. But most analytics teams treat adoption like an afterthought. "We built it, they should use it." No. Build it, make it irresistible, support the transition, measure adoption. Otherwise it's shelfware.

## The principle

Apply consumer behavior principles to analytics adoption. Make it stupid simple to use—reduce friction, eliminate training requirements where possible. Make the value obvious immediately—users should see ROI in their first session. Create habit loops—build daily use cases, send reminders, celebrate wins. Provide support—don't just launch and disappear, guide people through the transition. The magic happens when adoption feels inevitable because you designed the experience around human behavior, not just technical requirements. Less "we built the thing," more "we changed how people work." That's the game.

## The steps

1. Reduce Friction Ruthlessly: Map every step users take to get value. Where do they hesitate? Where do they give up? Eliminate those steps. Make it stupid simple.
2. Show Value in Session 1: Users should see ROI in their first session. Don't make them hunt for value. Put the win front and center. Make success immediate.
3. Build Habit Loops: Daily use cases, email reminders, weekly wins digests. Make using your tool a habit, not a chore. Celebrate wins publicly.

## In practice

A bank built a fraud detection dashboard for investigators. Usage: 15%. Why? Old process: investigators got alerts in email, clicked to a familiar system. New process: log into new dashboard, navigate complex UI, export data, paste into old system anyway. Too much friction. They rebuilt the experience: (1) Alerts still in email (met users where they were). (2) One-click from email to pre-filtered dashboard view (no navigation). (3) "Take Action" buttons right in the dashboard (closed the loop). (4) Weekly "wins" digest—"You stopped $50K in fraud this week using the dashboard." Adoption jumped to 78% in 10 weeks. Same tech, different behavior design. That's the difference.
