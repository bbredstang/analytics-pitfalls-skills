# Metric Overload Paralysis

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/35

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/35

## What it sounds like at work

"Can you just add one more metric?"

## The problem

Measuring everything or nothing. Three failure modes teams fall into: (1) Measure nothing—vibes and hopes guide decisions (2) Measure vanity metrics that don't change behavior—"we built 50 dashboards!" (3) Measure so much you have metric overload—37 metrics in a dashboard nobody can remember. The irony? Teams working on large-scale data transformation are VERY RARELY data-driven about themselves. If our data/analytics teams aren't data-driven, what hope does the rest of the organization have? Zero.

## The principle

HALO METRICS OVER METRIC SPAM: Measure just 3-5 metrics that have a SIGNIFICANT halo effect on how you operate. Metrics that are grounded in quarterly goals. Metrics that tell you the good, bad, AND ugly—not just successes. Metrics that have targets that shift but the metrics themselves don't change year over year. Metrics that bring CLARITY to current state.

Choose metrics that CHANGE behavior, not just report on it. Include uncomfortable truths—the best metrics reveal problems, not hide them. If you have 20 metrics, you have none. If metrics live in a deck nobody sees, they're worthless.

Do "BI on ourselves": Track usage, adoption, engagement in your own work. Review monthly as a team. Everyone should know the numbers. The test: If someone looked at your metrics and thought "How are they doing that? I want to work with them!" you're doing it right.

Less metrics. More focus. That's the path to actually being data-driven.

## The steps

1. Cut to 3-5 Halo Metrics: Audit all metrics. Keep only ones that: (a) Drive action, (b) Force good behavior, (c) Can't be gamed. Archive the rest.
2. Make Them Visible: Dashboard on the wall. Slack bot that posts them daily. Everyone should know the numbers without looking them up.
3. Monthly Team Review: Spend 30 minutes reviewing the metrics. What changed? Why? What are we doing about it? Make it a ritual.

## In practice

An analytics org tracked 37 metrics every month. Massive dashboard. Beautiful design. Nobody could remember what half of them meant.

Every monthly review became: "Wait, what does this one measure again?" "Is higher better or worse?" "Why did this drop?" "I don't know, let me check the definition..."

Nothing changed behavior because everything was important, which meant nothing was important.

New leader came in: "We're simplifying to 3 metrics. That's it."

The 3 chosen: (1) Solution usage rate—what percentage of our solutions get used weekly by 5+ people? (2) Time-to-first-value—how long from idea to production? (3) Team skill growth—are personal averages rising?

These three forced everything else to be good. High usage meant good requirements, good design, good data quality. Fast time-to-value meant streamlined process. Rising averages meant effective coaching and learning culture.

Within two quarters, all three improved 40%+ because the whole team could focus on what actually mattered. The 37-metric dashboard? Deleted. Nobody missed it.

Simplicity won. Again.
