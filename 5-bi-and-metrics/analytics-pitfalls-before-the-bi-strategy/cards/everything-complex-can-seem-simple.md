# Everything Complex Can Seem Simple (The Art of Ruthless Simplification)

A principle card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/25

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work could use this principle.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/25

## The problem

Your team has 47 metrics to track business health. Leadership is confused. Meetings devolve into "which number is right?" debates. Or your data model has 800 tables with cryptic names and nobody knows what's in them. Or your ML model has 93 features and even you can't explain how it works. Complexity creeps in like weeds in a garden—left unchecked, it chokes everything. And here's the trap: smart people love complexity. It feels impressive. But complexity kills adoption, slows action, and creates fragility. Every complex system started simple and got complicated by well-meaning people adding "just one more thing." The path to powerhouse status is not more complexity—it's ruthless simplification.

## The principle

Relentlessly simplify. Every metric, model, and process—ask: "Can this be simpler?" Reduce 47 metrics to 5 that people actually act on. Rename tables from "sls_txn_f47" to "sales_transactions." Cut features in your model from 93 to 10 that matter. Make things so simple they feel obvious. Think manufacturing: the best production lines are beautifully simple—raw materials flow in, finished products flow out, nothing wasted. Same with analytics. The magic happens when a new hire can understand your system in a week instead of six months. When executives trust the data because it's clear. When adoption is high because it's easy. Stupidly simple always wins.

## The steps

1. Apply the 60-Second Test: If you can't explain your strategy/goal/metric in 60 seconds to someone new, it's too complex. Simplify until you can.
2. Rename Everything in Plain English: Kill the jargon. "sls_txn_f47" becomes "sales_transactions_fact." Every metric gets a name your grandmother would understand.
3. Reduce 47 Metrics to 5: Audit everything you track. Keep only metrics someone acts on. Archive the rest. If you have >10 metrics, you have none.

## In practice

A financial services firm had 847 reports. Nobody knew which to trust. Each executive had "their" version of revenue. New CFO said "This is insane. Simplify." They audited: 712 reports hadn't been opened in 90 days—deleted instantly. Remaining 135 consolidated into 15 core reports with clear ownership and one source of truth. Revenue went from "depends who you ask" to "here's the number, one source, updated daily." People acted on the numbers twice as fast. But simplification didn't stop there: renamed metrics from jargon to plain English, rebuilt dashboards to show 5 KPIs instead of 50, trained everyone on the new standards. Six months later, a board member said "I finally understand this business." That's the power of simplicity.
