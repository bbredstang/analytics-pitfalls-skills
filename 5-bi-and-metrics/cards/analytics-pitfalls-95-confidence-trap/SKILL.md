# The 95% Confidence Trap (Perfection is the Enemy of Progress)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Auction Block* by Brian Cariveau (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/book).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/3

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/3

## What it sounds like at work

"Let’s wait until the data is perfect."

## The problem

Your team has been "analyzing" a launch decision for 6 months. You've run 47 different models, debated methodology in 23 meetings, and you're still not ready because "we need more data to be sure." Meanwhile, your competitor shipped 3 months ago, learned from real customers, iterated twice, and now owns the market. You were chasing 95% confidence when 70% was good enough to learn. It's like a manufacturing plant that won't ship a product until it's 100% perfect—so nothing ever ships. Paralysis by analysis. The irony? All that extra analysis didn't make you smarter—it made you slower. And in business, slow is expensive.

## The principle

Ship at 70% confidence and learn fast. Set a decision deadline and work backward. Define "good enough" data quality upfront—not perfect, good enough. The world rewards speed + learning over perfection. Think manufacturing: you don't wait until you have perfect raw materials to start the line. You start production, monitor quality, and adjust. Same with decisions. Make the call, instrument the hell out of it, watch what happens, and iterate. Slow down just enough to not be reckless, then speed up. The magic happens when teams get comfortable with uncertainty and learn to course-correct in real-time instead of over-planning in conference rooms.

## The steps

1. Set Decision Deadlines: For any analysis or launch decision, set a hard deadline. Work backward. Define "good enough" upfront - not perfect, good enough.
2. Ship at 70% with Kill Switches: Launch to 10% of users with monitoring on 5 key metrics. If it tanks, kill it fast. If it works, scale it. Learn in weeks, not months.
3. Track Decision Speed: Measure time from "we need to decide this" to "decision made." If it's >2 weeks for most decisions, you're overthinking. Speed up.

## In practice

A product team delayed a feature launch for 6 months seeking 95% confidence that it would drive engagement. Ran A/B test simulations, customer surveys, focus groups, competitive analysis. Meanwhile, competitor shipped a similar feature in 6 weeks. Customers loved it. By the time the overthinking team finally launched, the market had moved on. New VP came in, instituted a rule: "Ship at 70% confidence with kill switches." Next feature? Shipped in 3 weeks with monitoring to track 5 key metrics. Feature underperformed—but they knew in 2 weeks, not 6 months. Killed it, learned, shipped v2 in another 3 weeks. That one crushed it. Six months later they'd shipped 8 features vs the competitor's 2, learned what worked, and owned the category.
