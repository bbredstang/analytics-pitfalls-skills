# The Drain at the Top (When the Boss Still Wants the Old Report)

A pitfall card from Analytics Pitfalls & Principles, drawn from *The Second Factory*, Brian Cariveau's next novel (https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/second-factory).
https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/78

## How to use this card

Use it when what someone is working on, or something they heard at work, looks like the problem below: a plan, a roadmap, a request, a dashboard, a metric, an AI project.

1. If you don't know their situation yet (what they're working on, and where: their kind of company and team), ask one short question first, unless the conversation already says. Then fit everything below to it, not to a generic company.
2. Name the card by its title and say in a sentence why it fits. Say the work looks like this pitfall; never tell someone they have it.
3. Give the principle in the card's own words. Don't rewrite it.
4. Take the steps one at a time against their actual situation: what is already done, what isn't, and which to do first. If you need a fact to do that, ask one question, not five.
5. Then push past the technical fix, if the work needs people to work differently (most does). The steps are the if-then part. Ask what has to change in how people work, what they have to learn or believe, or what they give up, for it to stick. That's the adaptive work (Ronald Heifetz's term), and it's usually the real work.
6. If the card doesn't fit, say so plainly. Don't stretch it.
7. Finish with the card's link: https://www.analyticspitfalls.com/cards/78

## The problem

People come out of the AI session on fire. Then they go back to their desks, and on Monday their director asks for the weekly numbers in the old template. Their VP wants the same Friday deck. They can build it in ten minutes now instead of three hours. They still build it.

"My boss has never once used it," a customer service lead tells you. "So why would I change how I work for him?"

You thought people were sliding back out of habit. Some were. But much of the drain is what leaders keep asking for. And when you finally ask the leadership team to put a dot on the ladder where they honestly are, most of the dots land on the bottom rung.

## The principle

The drain goes all the way to the top. If you want to change what hundreds of people do, change what their leaders ask for.

Leaders who sponsor AI and never use it teach everyone that it's optional. Leaders who ask for the same reports the same way pull people straight back to the old work. So put the leaders on the ladder first, honestly, starting with the most senior person in the room.

Then ask each leader to name three things they request every week, and the real question behind each one. Stop asking for one report. Start asking for the answer, and what the person would do about it.

## The steps

1. **Put your own dot on the ladder.** Dabbler, daily user, builder, multiplier. Honestly. Then ask your leadership team to do the same, in front of each other, with the most senior person going first.
2. **Find the question behind the report.** Each leader names three things they ask for every week and writes down the real question behind each one.
3. **Retire one report each.** Every leader commits to stop asking for one report and to ask a question instead. Take a photo of the commitments.
4. **Ask for the answer and the action.** Not the deck. The answer, and what the person would do about it. A page people can click through and question beats a polished slide.

## In practice

After the sessions at ClassicMotors, the data team's chart kept showing people sliding back. When Nora Berg sat with them, she kept hearing the same thing. "I tried to do the weekly numbers the new way. My director sent it back. She wanted it in the old template."

So she asked the CEO for his whole leadership team, for two days, away from the building, and asked him to come as a participant. At an inn in Monterey, she drew a five-rung ladder on the whiteboard, from Dabbler up through Daily User, Builder and Multiplier, and asked each leader to put a dot where they honestly were.

The CEO went first and put his dot on the bottom rung. "I've used it to write two emails and plan a vacation. That's it. I told nine hundred people to become AI native, and I'm a dabbler." When they were done, seventeen of the twenty dots were on the bottom rung.

The next morning she asked them to write down three things they asked their teams for every week, and the real question behind each. The VP of Finance wrote: "Monthly variance pack, 40 pages. What I actually want to know: which three numbers surprised us and why."
